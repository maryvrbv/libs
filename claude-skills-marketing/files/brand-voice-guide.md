## Skill: Brand Voice Guide

A brand voice guide defines how your brand sounds across every touchpoint — website, email, social, support, product UI, and sales materials. Marketers write this to ensure consistency as the team grows, prevent brand drift across channels, and give writers and designers a clear reference for what sounds right and what doesn't. It answers: "If our brand were a person, how would they talk?"

### How to Write Each Section

**Brand Personality**
Write a character sketch, not a set of adjectives. Describe the person your brand would be: their background, how they'd talk at a dinner party, what makes them credible. This grounds every voice decision in something concrete.

Good: "Ledger sounds like a senior finance professional who left corporate to start their own advisory — someone who's been in the room when CFOs made hard calls, speaks with earned authority, but doesn't talk down to anyone. Direct, not stiff. Knows the technical details but explains them in plain terms."

Bad: "We are professional, innovative, and customer-centric." (Generic. Describes every company.)

**Voice Attributes**
Choose 3-4 attributes. Each one needs two definitions: what it means and what it doesn't mean. This prevents misinterpretation. "Friendly" doesn't mean "casual." "Confident" doesn't mean "arrogant." "Simple" doesn't mean "dumbed down." The "doesn't mean" column is where the real guidance lives.

**Do/Don't Examples**
For each voice attribute, show a specific example of on-brand and off-brand copy. These should be realistic sentences someone might actually write — not extreme caricatures. Include the "why" to explain the principle behind the correction.

**Tone Spectrum**
Voice is who you are. Tone is how you adapt to context. Map specific contexts to warmth and formality levels on a 1-5 scale, with example sentences. An error message should be warmer and less formal than a homepage headline. A support email should be warmer than a product tour.

**Vocabulary**
Specific word choices carry brand meaning. Define terms you use and terms you avoid, with reasons and alternatives. This is especially important for industry jargon — decide which terms you'll use, which you'll translate, and which you'll avoid entirely.

**Rewrite Pairs**
These are the most practical section. Show 5-8 before/after examples of real copy being revised to match the brand voice. Writers will reference these pairs more than any other section.

**Channel-Specific Adaptations**
The same voice shows up differently on Twitter vs. a product error message vs. a sales email. Define specific guidelines for each channel: length, tone shifts, formatting, and style preferences.

**Grammar & Mechanics**
Make style decisions once so writers don't have to decide every time. Oxford comma or not. Contractions or not. Exclamation point policy. Emoji policy. These seem minor, but inconsistency in mechanics erodes the feeling of a coherent brand.

### Conventions

- Length: 3-6 pages
- Tone: Clear and instructive — this document teaches people how to write in your voice
- Maximum 4 voice attributes — more than that and writers can't keep them all in mind while writing
- Every voice attribute needs "what it means" AND "what it doesn't mean" — the latter prevents misinterpretation
- Include at least 5 rewrite pairs — these are the most referenced section by writers
- The tone spectrum should cover at least 5 contexts with specific examples
- Include grammar and mechanics — these prevent style inconsistencies that make the brand feel fragmented
- Update this document when the brand evolves — voice should be reviewed annually at minimum
- Distribute this document to every person who writes on behalf of the brand — including agencies, contractors, and AI tools
- Include channel-specific guidance — the same voice adapts differently to a tweet vs. a product error message
- Use real copy examples from your actual product and marketing — not hypothetical examples
- Link to the positioning framework and messaging architecture — voice should be consistent with positioning

### Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT define voice with only positive attributes ("friendly, professional, innovative") — without "what it doesn't mean," every writer will interpret these differently
- Do NOT confuse voice with tone — voice is constant (who you are), tone varies by context (how you adapt). Define both
- Do NOT skip the rewrite pairs — abstract principles are hard to apply. Before/after examples make the voice tangible
- Do NOT write a voice guide and leave it in a shared drive — put it where writers work. Link it in your CMS, style guide, and onboarding docs
- Do NOT let the voice guide become a novel — if writers can't absorb it in 15 minutes, they won't use it
- Do NOT define vocabulary without reasons — "Don't say 'leverage'" is a rule without understanding. "Don't say 'leverage' because it's corporate jargon that makes us sound like every other B2B company — say 'use' instead" is guidance
- Do NOT make every channel sound identical — a tweet should not read like an error message. The voice is the same, but the tone and format adapt
- Do NOT use your voice guide for one-time campaigns only — it should be the reference for everything: product copy, support scripts, internal communications, and AI prompt engineering
- Do NOT skip the grammar and mechanics section — inconsistent capitalization, punctuation, and formatting make a brand feel amateur even when the messaging is strong
- Do NOT write the voice guide in committee — one person writes the draft, stakeholders review. Voice defined by consensus is voice defined by no one
- Do NOT treat the voice guide as a creative brief — it's a reference document. Keep it structured, scannable, and example-heavy
- Do NOT ignore product UI in the voice guide — button labels, error messages, and empty states are some of the most-read copy your brand produces

### Examples

```markdown
# Brand Voice Guide: Ledger Financial

**Author:** Nora Chen
**Last Updated:** 2026-03-25
**Status:** Approved

---

## 1. Brand Personality

Ledger sounds like a senior financial advisor who left a big firm to build something better — someone who's sat across the table from CFOs making hard decisions, earned their credibility through results, and now uses plain language because they've outgrown the need to impress with jargon. They're the person you trust with your money because they explain exactly what they're doing and why, without talking down to you. Direct but never cold. Knowledgeable but never condescending.

## 2. Voice Attributes

| Attribute | What It Means | What It Doesn't Mean |
|-----------|--------------|---------------------|
| Clear | We use short sentences, common words, and precise language. If a concept is complex, we break it down — we don't hide behind jargon | Not dumbed down. We respect our audience's intelligence. We simplify language, not ideas |
| Confident | We state facts directly. No hedging, no "we believe" or "we think." We've done the work and we stand behind it | Not arrogant. We're confident in our expertise, not dismissive of questions or alternatives |
| Warm | We acknowledge the human side of financial decisions — the stress, the stakes, the relief when it works. We're approachable | Not casual. We're warm, not buddy-buddy. Financial decisions are serious and we treat them that way |
| Precise | We use specific numbers, timeframes, and outcomes. We avoid vague language ("many," "significant," "robust") | Not dry. Precision doesn't mean clinical. We can be precise and still sound like a human being |

## 3. Do / Don't Examples

### Clear

**Do:**
> Your cash flow forecast updates every morning at 8 AM. Open the dashboard to see where you stand.

**Don't:**
> Leverage our real-time financial analytics capabilities to gain actionable visibility into your organization's cash flow trajectory.

**Why:** The first sentence tells you what happens, when, and what to do about it. The second says the same thing in twice as many words with zero additional clarity.

### Confident

**Do:**
> Ledger reduces month-end close from 12 days to 4. Here's how.

**Don't:**
> We believe Ledger can help potentially streamline your month-end close process and may reduce the timeline significantly.

**Why:** Hedging ("we believe," "potentially," "may") signals uncertainty. If we have the data, state it. If we don't, get the data.

### Warm

**Do:**
> Month-end close is stressful. We built Ledger so you can finish it by Wednesday and actually enjoy your weekend.

**Don't:**
> Ledger optimizes your month-end close workflow for maximum efficiency and resource utilization.

**Why:** The first version acknowledges the pain (stress, lost weekends) and connects the product to a human outcome. The second sounds like a press release.

### Precise

**Do:**
> Ledger customers close their books 67% faster. The median close dropped from 12 days to 4 days across our customer base (n=340).

**Don't:**
> Ledger significantly accelerates your close process, helping many customers achieve much faster results.

**Why:** "Significantly," "many," and "much faster" communicate nothing specific. The reader has to trust you blindly. Numbers earn trust.

## 4. Tone Spectrum

_Voice is constant. Tone adapts to context._

| Context | Tone Shift | Warmth | Formality | Example |
|---------|-----------|--------|-----------|---------|
| Marketing website | Confident, outcome-focused. Lead with results | 3 | 3 | "Close your books in 4 days, not 12. Ledger automates the work so your team can focus on analysis." |
| Email marketing | Slightly warmer, conversational | 4 | 2 | "Quick question: how many hours did your team spend on last month's close? Most of our customers cut that in half within 60 days." |
| Social media (LinkedIn) | Thought leadership, insight-driven | 3 | 3 | "The average mid-market company spends 12 days closing their books each month. That's 144 days per year spent looking backward. What if you could reclaim 96 of those days?" |
| Social media (Twitter/X) | Sharper, punchier, more opinionated | 3 | 2 | "Month-end close takes 12 days at most companies. Ours takes 4. The difference isn't headcount. It's automation." |
| Product UI — buttons & labels | Action-oriented, minimal | 2 | 3 | "Run reconciliation" / "Export report" / "Invite team member" |
| Product UI — empty states | Helpful, encouraging | 4 | 2 | "No transactions imported yet. Connect your bank account to get started — it takes about 2 minutes." |
| Error messages | Calm, blame-free, action-oriented | 4 | 2 | "We couldn't connect to your bank. This usually means the session expired. Try reconnecting — it takes 30 seconds." |
| Customer support | Empathetic, solution-focused | 5 | 2 | "I can see why that's frustrating — a reconciliation error right before close is stressful. Here's what happened and how we'll fix it." |
| Sales emails | Consultative, specific to their situation | 4 | 3 | "I noticed your team is 85 people across 3 entities. Companies at that scale typically spend 10-14 days on month-end close. Want to see how Ledger cuts that to 4?" |

## 5. Vocabulary

### Words We Use
| Word/Phrase | Why We Use It | Instead Of |
|-------------|--------------|-----------|
| "Close your books" | Specific financial language our audience uses daily | "Process your financials" |
| "Automate" | Clear, specific about what we do | "Streamline" (vague) |
| "Your team" | Focuses on the people doing the work | "Your organization" (corporate) |
| "Connect" | For integrations — implies simplicity | "Integrate" (sounds technical) |
| "In [X] days" | Specific timeframe signals precision | "Quickly" or "fast" (vague) |
| "Here's how" | Invites the reader in, signals transparency | "Learn more" (passive, generic) |

### Words We Avoid
| Word/Phrase | Why We Avoid It | Use This Instead |
|-------------|----------------|-----------------|
| "Leverage" | Corporate jargon that adds no meaning | "Use" |
| "Best-in-class" | Unprovable superlative | Specific claim: "67% faster close" |
| "Robust" | Meaningless filler | Name the specific capability |
| "Seamless" | Nothing is seamless — overclaiming erodes trust | "Takes 2 minutes to set up" |
| "Stakeholders" | Dehumanizing — these are people | "Your CEO," "your team," "your auditors" |
| "Synergy" | Corporate cliche that signals empty thinking | Describe the specific benefit of working together |
| "Revolutionary" | Hyperbole. Let results speak | State the measurable improvement |
| "End-to-end" | Vague — end of what to end of what? | Name the specific start and end points |

| "Don't hesitate to reach out if you have any questions!" | "Questions? Email us at help@ledger.com — we reply within 2 hours." | Replaced the generic "don't hesitate" with a specific action and response-time commitment |

## 7. Channel-Specific Adaptations

### Website
- **Headlines:** 8 words or fewer. Sentence case. Lead with the outcome, not the product name
- **Body copy:** 2-3 sentence paragraphs maximum. Reading level: Grade 8-10. Use bullet points for lists of 3+ items
- **CTAs:** Start with an action verb. Max 4 words. "Start free trial" not "Click here to get started with your free trial"

### Email
- **Subject lines:** 6-10 words. No ALL CAPS. No clickbait. Ask a question or state a benefit
- **Body:** 150-250 words max for marketing emails. One CTA per email. Use short paragraphs (1-2 sentences)
- **Sign-off:** "— [First name], Ledger" for marketing. Full name and title for sales

### Social Media
- **Twitter/X:** Use the full 280 characters when you have something to say. No hashtags except for events. Threads: max 5 tweets. End threads with a clear next step, not "like and retweet"
- **LinkedIn:** 150-300 words per post. Open with a hook (first 2 lines visible before "see more"). End with a question or insight, not a sales pitch. No emoji in the first line
- **Instagram:** Not a primary channel. When used: pair product screenshots with 1-2 sentence captions. No stock photography

### Product UI
- **Button labels:** 2-3 words, active voice. "Run report" not "Click to run your report." Verb + object format
- **Empty states:** Explain what will appear here and give a one-step action to populate it. Tone: helpful, not apologetic
- **Error messages:** Never blame the user. State what happened, why, and what they can do. Example: "We couldn't save your changes. The connection timed out. Try again — it usually works on the second attempt."
- **Success messages:** Brief and specific. "Report exported. Check your downloads folder." Not "Congratulations! Your report has been successfully exported!"

### Customer Support
- **Opening:** "Hi [First name]," — never "Dear valued customer" or "Hello there"
- **Issue acknowledgment:** Validate first, solve second. "I can see the reconciliation ran twice — that's definitely not right."
- **Resolution:** Explain what happened and what we did about it. Not just "it's fixed"
- **Closing:** "Let me know if anything else comes up — happy to help." Never "Please do not hesitate to reach out"

## 8. Grammar & Mechanics

- **Oxford comma:** Yes, always
- **Contractions:** Use them in all channels except legal documents and formal partnership communications. "You'll" not "You will." "We're" not "We are." Contractions sound human
- **Capitalization:** Sentence case for all headings and subheadings. Title case only for the product name (Ledger) and proper nouns. Feature names are lowercase ("bank reconciliation," not "Bank Reconciliation")
- **Numbers:** Use digits for all numbers, including 1-9. "$4M" not "$4 million." "3 days" not "three days." Exception: "one" when used as a pronoun ("one of the best")
- **Exclamation points:** Maximum one per email or page. Never in headlines. Never in error messages. Reserve for genuine celebration ("Your first month-end close with Ledger: 4 days. Nice work.")
- **Emoji:** Not used in website copy, product UI, or email marketing. Acceptable sparingly on Twitter/X and LinkedIn (max 1 per post, never in the first line)
- **Ampersands:** Only in headers when space is tight. "and" in all body copy
- **Abbreviations:** Spell out on first use, then abbreviate. Exception: universally known terms (API, CFO, ROI) don't need to be spelled out
```

### Customization

Replace all `[bracketed]` sections with your brand specifics.
