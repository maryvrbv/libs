## Skill: Campaign Retrospective

A campaign retrospective is the post-mortem analysis a marketer writes after a campaign ends. It captures what worked, what did not, and what the team should do differently next time. A marketer writes this within one week of campaign completion, while the data is fresh and the team remembers the decisions behind the numbers.

### How to Write Each Section

**Campaign Overview**
Set the context so anyone reading this — including someone who was not involved — understands what the campaign was and why it existed. The objective should be specific and measurable: "Generate 500 MQLs for the enterprise segment at under $60 CPL" not "raise awareness." The target audience should name a specific segment, not "our customers." The brief summary should explain the concept (what was the creative hook), the offer (what was the CTA or incentive), and the mechanism (how the campaign reached people).

Good objective: "Generate 300 trial signups for ProSchedule Teams among marketing managers at companies with 50-500 employees, at a CAC under $80."
Bad objective: "Launch the Teams product and get signups."

Good audience: "Marketing managers and directors at B2B SaaS companies with 50-500 employees, primarily in the US and UK, who are currently using a competitor or no tool."
Bad audience: "Marketing professionals."

**Goals vs. Results**
This is the accountability table. List every metric that was defined as a goal before the campaign launched. Show the goal, actual result, percentage of goal achieved, and a clear verdict. Be direct in the overall verdict — "The campaign missed its primary MQL target by 22% but exceeded pipeline targets by 15% because lead quality was higher than expected" is useful. "The campaign had mixed results" is not.

Include 4-6 metrics spanning different dimensions: volume (leads, signups), quality (SQL rate, pipeline value), efficiency (CPL, CAC, ROAS), and engagement (CTR, open rate, conversion rate).

**What Worked**
Organize by tactic or decision, not by channel. Each subsection should explain what happened, why it worked, and cite specific evidence. This is where institutional knowledge gets built. A future marketer reading this should be able to replicate the success.

Good: "LinkedIn carousel ads outperformed single-image by 2.3x on CTR and 1.8x on conversion rate. The carousel format let us walk through the before/after workflow in 4 slides, which matched how our audience evaluates tools — they want to see the process, not just the promise. We tested 3 carousel sequences and the one leading with the pain point (manual scheduling) beat the one leading with the feature (AI scheduling) by 40%."
Bad: "LinkedIn did well."

**What Didn't Work**
Same structure as What Worked, but for failures. Be honest and analytical, not defensive. The goal is to prevent the same mistake next time. Diagnose the root cause — was it the targeting, the creative, the offer, the timing, or the channel?

Good: "The Meta lookalike audience based on free trial users generated a 0.3% CTR vs. our 1.2% benchmark. Root cause: the seed audience (free trial users) includes a large percentage of individual users and freelancers who do not match the enterprise target for this campaign. Next time: build the lookalike from paid customers in the target segment, not all trial users."
Bad: "Facebook ads didn't really work for this one."

**Channel-by-Channel Breakdown**
One row per channel. Include spend, volume metrics, conversion metrics, efficiency metrics, and a notes column for the key observation. The notes column is critical — it is where you capture the insight that the numbers alone do not tell. "CTR dropped 40% in week 3 due to creative fatigue" or "Best performing day was Tuesday, worst was Friday."

**Audience Insights**
What did the data reveal about who responded to this campaign? This is different from who you targeted — it is who actually engaged. Look at demographic breakdowns, geographic patterns, device usage, time-of-day patterns, and any segment-level differences. Compare the audience that converted to the audience you assumed would convert.

**Creative Insights**
What did the data reveal about which messages, formats, and visuals worked? Include A/B test results if you ran them. Note patterns: did problem-focused copy beat feature-focused copy? Did video outperform static? Did long-form outperform short-form? These insights inform the creative brief for the next campaign.

**Budget Analysis**
Compare planned spend to actual spend by category. Calculate cost efficiency metrics (CPL, CPA, ROAS) and compare them to your benchmarks and to previous campaigns. If the campaign was expensive but generated high-quality leads that closed at a high rate, say so — efficiency is not just about cost per lead.

**Recommendations**
This is the most important section. Organize into four categories: Do Again (proven tactics to repeat), Do Differently (tactics that need adjustment), Stop Doing (tactics to cut), Start Doing (new hypotheses to test). Each recommendation should be specific and actionable, not vague. "Test video ads on LinkedIn" is better than "explore new formats."

### Conventions

- Write the retrospective within 7 days of campaign completion, while data and context are fresh.
- Include only final, verified numbers. If attribution is still settling (common for pipeline metrics), note the data freshness and plan to update.
- Compare results to both goals and historical benchmarks. A campaign that hit its goal but performed worse than the last three campaigns is still a concern.
- Use the same metric definitions consistently. If "conversion" means "trial signup" in this campaign, say so explicitly.
- Attribution model: State which attribution model you used (first-touch, last-touch, multi-touch, linear). Different models tell different stories.
- Round financial figures to meaningful precision. $12,450 is fine. $12,447.83 is noise.
- Name the team. Credit people who contributed to wins. Name owners for follow-up actions.
- Link to the campaign brief, creative assets, and analytics dashboards rather than reproducing raw data.
- Include screenshots of top-performing and worst-performing creative when possible — visual evidence is more memorable than descriptions.
- Archive the retrospective in a shared location where future campaign planners can reference it.
- Length: 150-250 lines. Comprehensive but not exhaustive. Every section earns its place.
- Tone: Analytical and honest. Not defensive, not celebratory. Report reality and extract learnings.

### Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT write the retrospective without looking at the data. "I think the campaign went well" without numbers is a journal entry, not an analysis.
- Do NOT blame external factors without evidence. "The algorithm changed" is a hypothesis, not a finding. Show the data that supports or refutes it.
- Do NOT skip the "What Didn't Work" section. Every campaign has failures. If you cannot identify them, you are not looking hard enough.
- Do NOT list vanity metrics (impressions, reach) as primary results. These are inputs, not outcomes.
- Do NOT compare results to goals that were set after the campaign launched. If the goal was 500 MQLs and you got 200, the goal was not "actually 200."
- Do NOT write recommendations that are too vague to act on. "Improve targeting" is not a recommendation. "Build lookalike audiences from closed-won customers in the 50-500 employee segment" is.
- Do NOT ignore the budget analysis. Understanding cost efficiency is as important as understanding performance.
- Do NOT wait more than 2 weeks to write the retrospective. Context decay is real — decisions and rationale become fuzzy fast.
- Do NOT write a retrospective that only the marketing team can read. Cross-functional stakeholders (sales, product, leadership) should be able to understand the findings without a decoder ring.
- Do NOT confuse correlation with causation. "We increased LinkedIn spend and MQLs went up" does not mean LinkedIn caused the increase unless you controlled for other variables.
- Do NOT forget to document what you would do differently. The retrospective exists to improve the next campaign, not to archive the last one.
- Do NOT omit the attribution model. "This campaign generated 400 leads" means different things under first-touch vs. multi-touch attribution.
- Do NOT treat the retrospective as a one-time document. Reference it when planning the next campaign. Build on the learnings.
- Do NOT include raw data dumps. Summarize the insights. Link to the raw data for anyone who wants to drill down.

### Examples

#### Campaign Retrospective: ProSchedule Teams Launch

**Product Context:** ProSchedule is a scheduling tool for marketing teams. The Teams plan is a new tier targeting marketing departments at mid-market companies (50-500 employees). The launch campaign aimed to generate trial signups and pipeline for the sales team.

---

```markdown
# ProSchedule Teams Launch — Retrospective

**Campaign Dates:** February 3 — February 28, 2026
**Author:** Marcus Webb, Growth Marketing Lead
**Date Written:** 2026-03-05
**Campaign Type:** Product Launch

---

## Campaign Overview

**Objective:** Generate 400 trial signups for ProSchedule Teams among marketing managers at companies with 50-500 employees, at a CAC under $80. Secondary goal: generate $500K in sales pipeline.

**Target Audience:** Marketing managers and directors at B2B SaaS companies with 50-500 employees in the US and UK. Specifically targeting teams currently using Google Calendar, Calendly, or a competitor scheduling tool for team coordination.

**Channels Used:** LinkedIn Ads, Google Search, Email (existing list + partner newsletters), Organic social (LinkedIn, Twitter), Product Hunt launch (day 1).

**Total Budget:** $32,000

**Total Duration:** 26 days

**Brief Summary:** The campaign launched ProSchedule Teams with a "Stop playing calendar Tetris" concept, positioning the product as the upgrade from individual scheduling tools to team-wide coordination. The offer was a 30-day free trial with white-glove onboarding for teams of 5+. Distribution combined paid acquisition (LinkedIn, Google), owned channels (email, social), and a Product Hunt launch on day one.

## Goals vs. Results

| Metric | Goal | Actual | % of Goal | Verdict |
|--------|------|--------|-----------|---------|
| Trial Signups | 400 | 342 | 86% | Missed |
| Sales Pipeline Generated | $500K | $620K | 124% | Exceeded |
| Cost Per Trial Signup | <$80 | $94 | 85% (inverted) | Missed |
| Trial-to-Paid Conversion | 12% | 18% | 150% | Exceeded |
| LinkedIn Ad CTR | 1.5% | 2.1% | 140% | Exceeded |
| Email Open Rate (Launch Sequence) | 35% | 41% | 117% | Exceeded |

**Overall Verdict:** The campaign missed its primary volume target (342 vs. 400 trial signups) and ran over on CPA ($94 vs. $80 target). However, lead quality significantly exceeded expectations — 18% trial-to-paid conversion vs. 12% target — which resulted in pipeline exceeding the goal by 24%. Net assessment: the campaign prioritized quality over quantity, which was the right trade-off for a new product launch. Volume targets should have been set lower with higher quality benchmarks.

## What Worked

### Product Hunt Launch Drove High-Quality Early Adopters
Product Hunt generated 89 trial signups on launch day at $0 cost (organic listing). These users had the highest trial-to-paid conversion rate of any channel at 24%. The upvote campaign we ran via email to existing users (asking them to support on PH) generated 340 upvotes and a #4 Product of the Day finish. This front-loaded the campaign with momentum and gave us social proof to reference in paid ads ("Rated #4 on Product Hunt").

### LinkedIn Carousel Ads Outperformed All Paid Creative
The LinkedIn carousel format showing a 4-step "before/after" workflow (manual scheduling chaos vs. ProSchedule Teams coordination) generated a 2.1% CTR — 40% above our 1.5% benchmark. The carousel format matched how the audience evaluates workflow tools: they want to see the process, not just the end state. We tested 3 carousel sequences; the one leading with the pain point ("Your team wastes 4.7 hours/week on scheduling") beat the feature-led sequence ("AI-powered team scheduling") by 35% on CTR and 28% on conversion.

### Email Segmentation by Current Tool
We segmented the launch email by the scheduling tool each contact currently uses (pulled from enrichment data). The "Calendly users" segment received copy specifically addressing Calendly's team limitations, while the "no tool" segment received copy about the cost of manual scheduling. The segmented emails had a 41% open rate vs. 28% for the generic version we sent to unsegmented contacts. The segmented version also converted at 2.3x the rate of the generic version.

## What Didn't Work

### Google Search — Broad Match Keywords Burned Budget
We allocated $8,000 to Google Search targeting "team scheduling software," "marketing team calendar," and related terms. Broad match keywords triggered irrelevant queries — "free team calendar," "scheduling software for schools," "calendar app for families" — consuming $3,200 before we identified the issue on day 8 and switched to phrase/exact match. After the fix, Google Search CPA dropped from $140 to $72, but the early waste inflated the overall campaign CPA.

### Meta Lookalike Audience Missed the Mark
We built a Meta lookalike audience from ProSchedule free-tier users. The assumption was that free users represent our ideal customer profile. They do not. Free-tier users skew heavily toward freelancers and individual contributors — not the marketing managers at mid-market companies we targeted. The Meta campaign generated 28 trial signups at $142 CPA — 78% above target. The trial-to-paid conversion for these leads was 6%, less than half the campaign average.

## Audience Insights

- **Company size mattered more than job title.** Contacts at 100-300 employee companies converted at 2.4x the rate of contacts at 50-99 employee companies. The team coordination pain point is more acute once a marketing department has 5+ people.
- **Geography split:** US contacts converted at 14% trial-to-paid vs. 8% for UK contacts. The UK audience engaged with content but had a longer decision cycle — possibly due to different purchasing norms.
- **Device:** 72% of trial signups came from desktop. This is a workflow tool — people evaluate it during work hours on their work machines, not on mobile.
- **Day of week:** Tuesday and Wednesday drove 48% of all conversions. Monday and Friday were weakest. Future campaigns should front-load budget on Tue-Wed.
- **Unexpected segment:** 12% of signups came from customer success teams, not marketing teams. The scheduling pain point crosses departments.

## Creative Insights

- **Pain-led copy beat feature-led copy by 35% on CTR.** "Your team wastes 4.7 hours/week on scheduling" outperformed "AI-powered team scheduling" across every channel.
- **Carousel format outperformed single image by 2.3x on LinkedIn.** The process-oriented format matched how the audience evaluates tools.
- **Social proof lifted landing page conversion by 22%.** Adding the Product Hunt badge and "trusted by 2,400 marketing teams" to the landing page (added on day 3) increased conversion from 8.2% to 10.0%.
- **Video ads on LinkedIn underperformed static.** The 30-second product demo video had a 0.8% CTR vs. 2.1% for carousels. Hypothesis: this audience prefers to self-direct their evaluation rather than watch a prescribed demo.
- **Long-form email outperformed short-form.** The 400-word launch email with detailed feature breakdown converted at 1.8x the rate of the 120-word "check it out" version. This audience wants depth before committing to a trial.

**Cost Efficiency Assessment:** Overall CPA of $94 exceeded the $80 target by 18%. However, when accounting for trial-to-paid conversion (18% vs. 12% benchmark), the effective cost per paying customer was $522 — below the $667 target ($80 CPA / 12% conversion). The campaign was more efficient than the topline CPA suggests because it attracted higher-quality leads.

## Recommendations

### Do Again
- LinkedIn carousel ads with pain-point-first sequencing — proven format for this audience at scale
- Product Hunt launch for major product releases — high-quality leads at zero cost
- Email segmentation by current tool — the personalization drove measurably higher engagement and conversion
- Tuesday/Wednesday budget front-loading — match spend to the audience's evaluation pattern

### Do Differently
- Set trial signup targets based on lead quality tiers, not raw volume — this campaign showed that fewer, better leads can exceed pipeline goals
- Start Google Search with phrase/exact match only — test broad match in a separate, capped experiment rather than in the main campaign
- Build Meta lookalike audiences from paid customers in the target segment, not from all free users

### Stop Doing
- Meta lookalike audiences seeded from free-tier users for B2B campaigns — the audience mismatch is structural, not fixable with optimization
- 30-second product demo videos on LinkedIn for this audience — the format underperformed static by 2.6x

### Start Doing
- Test LinkedIn Thought Leader ads (boosting employee posts) — the organic LinkedIn posts from the CEO performed well; paid amplification could scale this
- Build a dedicated retargeting sequence for the 658 trial signups who did not convert — these are warm leads with demonstrated intent
- Test customer success teams as a secondary audience segment — the 12% unexpected signup rate suggests latent demand
```

### Customization

Replace all bracket sections with your actual campaign details. Adjust the channel breakdown to include only the channels you used. Add or remove metrics in the Goals vs. Results table to match what was defined in your campaign brief. The structure stays the same; the content is yours.

Reference previous retrospectives when planning new campaigns. The "Do Again" and "Do Differently" sections are the most valuable part of the archive — they compound learning across campaigns.
