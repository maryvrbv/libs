## Skill: Community Growth

A community growth playbook defines how you build, engage, and scale a community that drives acquisition, retention, and brand advocacy. Marketers write this to turn passive users into active participants who create value for each other — and for the business. It answers: "What kind of community do we build, where do we build it, how do we keep it alive, and how does it drive growth?"

### How to Write Each Section

**Community Strategy**
Start with purpose. If you can't articulate why someone would join and stay in one sentence, you don't have a community — you have a notification channel. The best communities exist for their members, not for the company. The business value is a byproduct of genuine member value. Define the community type — support, practice, networking, or advocacy — because each type requires different design, moderation, and content.

Good: "The ConnectAPI Community exists so API integration engineers can share patterns, troubleshoot issues, and stay current on best practices — without waiting for documentation updates or filing support tickets. Members get answers from practitioners who've built what they're building."

Bad: "Our community is a place for users to engage with our brand and each other." (No value proposition, no specificity.)

**Engagement Framework**
Accept the power law: 1-3% of members will create most of the value, 60-70% will lurk. Design for each level. For lurkers, reduce the barrier to first participation (polls, reactions, simple prompts). For contributors, recognize and reward their effort. For leaders, give them real responsibility and influence. The goal is to move people up the engagement ladder, not to force everyone to be a contributor.

**Content Programming**
Consistency matters more than brilliance. A community that posts a discussion prompt every Monday at 9 AM and showcases member work every Thursday builds a rhythm that members rely on. Sporadic bursts of activity followed by silence are worse than modest but consistent engagement. Plan content a month ahead. Assign owners to every content slot.

**Ambassador Program**
Your ambassadors are force multipliers. 10 great ambassadors can maintain a 2,000-person community. Select for intrinsic motivation — people who already contribute because they care, not people who want the title. Give them meaningful benefits (early access, direct product influence) not just swag. Set clear expectations — if an ambassador goes quiet for 30 days, have a conversation about whether they want to continue.

**Moderation**
Over-moderate and the community feels sterile. Under-moderate and it becomes toxic. Set clear guidelines upfront, enforce them consistently, and err on the side of kindness for first offenses (DM before public action). The community manager sets the tone — how they respond to the first conflict determines the community's culture.

### Conventions

- Length: 5-8 pages
- Tone: Warm but structured. Community work is part art, part system
- Start with a small, engaged core before scaling — 50 engaged members are worth more than 500 lurkers
- Personally welcome every new member for the first 500 members
- Post consistently — a dead community is worse than no community
- Measure engagement quality (depth of conversations) not just quantity (post count)
- Design for the lurkers — most members will never post, and that's okay
- Build the ambassador program when you have 500+ members and can identify natural leaders
- Use the community as a feedback channel for product — members are your most invested users
- Never let the community become a one-way announcement channel — if 90% of posts are from the company, it's not a community
- Separate community metrics from marketing metrics — community value compounds over months, not days
- Document community guidelines and enforce them from day one

### Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT launch a community without a plan to sustain it — an abandoned community is worse than no community. It signals the company doesn't care
- Do NOT treat the community as a support ticket deflection tool — if the only reason the community exists is to reduce your support costs, members will feel it and leave
- Do NOT let the company dominate the conversation — if 80% of posts are from official accounts, it's a broadcast channel, not a community
- Do NOT neglect moderation — one toxic member can drive away 100 good ones. Address issues early
- Do NOT rely on a platform without searchable history — if great answers disappear into a chat scroll, the community's knowledge base is lost
- Do NOT launch an ambassador program before you have natural leaders emerging — you can't manufacture ambassadors, you recognize and empower them
- Do NOT measure community success by member count alone — a 10,000-member community with 2% engagement is worse than a 500-member community with 30% engagement
- Do NOT scale before you've found community-market fit — if you don't know what content and formats drive engagement, scaling just amplifies confusion
- Do NOT ignore the onboarding experience — a new member who joins, sees no activity or welcome, and leaves in 5 minutes is gone forever
- Do NOT run a community part-time with no dedicated owner — communities need daily attention, not weekly check-ins
- Do NOT copy another company's community structure — what works for a developer community won't work for a designer community. Design for your specific audience
- Do NOT use the community primarily as a selling channel — members will leave the moment they feel sold to instead of served

### Content Principles
1. **Teach, don't promote.** Every post should make members better at their craft. If it's about CodeCraft the product, it's a product announcement — put it in #announcements, not #general.
2. **Celebrate members more than the product.** The weekly showcase features their work, not ours. When we share a member's project on Twitter, we tag them and let them shine.
3. **Every post invites a response.** "Here's a tip" is less engaging than "Here's a tip — what's yours?" Statements create broadcasts. Questions create conversations.
4. **Show the mess.** Perfect code screenshots are intimidating. Sharing the error message, the 3 AM debugging session, the ugly first version — that's what builds trust.
