## Skill: Content Calendar

A content calendar is the operational plan that turns a content strategy into a publishing schedule. It maps every piece of content to a date, channel, owner, and status — so nothing falls through the cracks and the team knows exactly what's shipping when. Without one, content strategy stays theoretical and publishing becomes reactive.

### How to Write Each Section

**Monthly Themes**
Every month should have a theme that ties content together and supports a strategic goal. Themes prevent random-acts-of-content. They should connect to business objectives — a product launch, a seasonal trend, an audience pain point.

Good: "April theme: Migration Season — help teams switching from legacy tools. Supports Q2 pipeline goal. Aligns with competitor contract renewal cycles."

Bad: "April theme: Spring cleaning" (vague, no strategic connection)

Pick themes 4-6 weeks ahead. Lock them. Changing themes mid-month means your content never compounds.

**Content Pillars**
Pillars define what you talk about, not what you sell. Every piece of content should map to exactly one pillar. If it maps to zero, it's off-brand. If it maps to three, it's unfocused. Allocate percentages to keep the mix intentional — if you notice 80% of your content is one pillar, you've lost balance.

Good: "Pillar: Workflow Automation — how teams eliminate repetitive tasks. 30% of content. Primary channel: Blog."

Bad: "Pillar: Our Product" (that's not a content pillar, that's a sales pitch)

**Weekly Calendar**
This is the operational view. Every slot has a content type, title, channel, owner, and status. Empty slots are fine — they're buffer for reactive content (trending topics, news). But more than 30% empty means you're not planning enough.

Status progression: Idea → Outline → Draft → Review → Approved → Scheduled → Published. Every piece should move through these stages. If something sits in "Draft" for more than a week, it's blocked — figure out why.

**Content Pipeline**
The pipeline is the backlog — everything in various stages of production, not just this week. Sort by target date. This is where you spot bottlenecks: if 8 pieces are in "Draft" and 0 are in "Review," you have an editing bottleneck, not a writing problem.

**Repurposing Map**
Every substantial piece of content (blog post, webinar, case study, report) should generate 3-5 derivative pieces. Map these explicitly. If you're creating original content for every channel, you're working too hard. The repurposing map makes this systematic instead of ad hoc.

**Recurring Content**
Anything that publishes on a fixed schedule goes here. Templates and processes should exist for each recurring item — if someone is sick, another person can execute. If a recurring content type doesn't have a template, create one before the next issue.

**Key Dates**
Product launches, industry events, holidays, competitor events, contract renewal cycles. Anything that requires content preparation. Work backward from the date — if a blog post needs 5 business days to produce, the deadline to start writing is marked here, not the publish date.

**Capacity**
Know how much your team can produce before you commit to a calendar. If your writer can produce 3 blog posts per week and you've scheduled 5, the calendar is fiction. Track capacity against load weekly.

### Conventions

- Update the calendar at the start of every week. A stale calendar is worse than no calendar.
- Lock content 1 week before publish date. After that, only fix errors — no rewriting.
- Every piece of content has exactly one owner. The owner is responsible for moving it through all stages.
- Use consistent status labels across the entire calendar. Do not invent new statuses.
- Pillar allocation percentages should add up to 100%.
- Leave 20% buffer in the calendar for reactive content (news, trends, opportunities).
- Time-sensitive content (event tie-ins, launches) gets locked 2 weeks out. Non-urgent content gets locked 1 week out.
- Review pillar distribution monthly. If one pillar is consistently over- or under-represented, adjust.
- File the calendar where the whole team can access it. A calendar only the content lead sees is a to-do list, not a collaboration tool.
- Include estimated production time for each content type so capacity planning is realistic.
- Archive completed weeks — don't delete them. Historical calendars are useful for performance reviews.

### Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT create a calendar without content pillars. Without pillars, you're scheduling random content with no strategic coherence.
- Do NOT schedule more content than your team can produce. An overloaded calendar leads to missed deadlines, rushed work, or both.
- Do NOT treat the calendar as immutable. Plans change — but change deliberately, not reactively. Document why something moved.
- Do NOT skip the repurposing map. Original content for every channel is unsustainable and unnecessary.
- Do NOT plan content without checking key dates. Missing a product launch because nobody told the content team is a coordination failure, not a content failure.
- Do NOT assign content without confirming capacity. "We'll figure it out" is not a resource plan.
- Do NOT leave the status column empty. If you don't know the status, that's the problem to solve.
- Do NOT mix content types and channels in a way that confuses the team. One row = one piece of content on one channel.
- Do NOT plan more than 6-8 weeks ahead in detail. Beyond that, use monthly themes and tentative slots. Detailed planning too far out wastes effort on content that will change.
- Do NOT publish without a distribution plan. Creating the content is half the work. If there's no plan for how it reaches the audience, it won't.
- Do NOT ignore performance data when planning the next period. Check what worked last month before deciding what to create this month.
- Do NOT create a calendar in one tool and publish from another without syncing. The calendar is the single source of truth for what's going live when.
- Do NOT schedule all content on the same day. Spread publishing across the week for consistent presence.
