### How to Write Each Section

**Issue Strategy:** Every issue needs a theme — one angle that ties everything together. A newsletter without a theme is a random list of links. The theme could be a topic ("retention metrics"), a format ("3 things I learned this week"), or a narrative ("the mistake I made last quarter").

Good: "Theme: Why most marketing dashboards lie. Content mix: 70% original analysis of vanity metrics vs. actionable metrics, 20% curated links to dashboard tools that do it right, 10% soft mention of our analytics product."

Bad: "Theme: Marketing stuff. We'll include some links and talk about our product."

**Subject Line & Preview:** The subject line determines open rate. It should be specific enough to set expectations but curiosity-provoking enough to earn the click. Preview text is the second line of the pitch — use it to add new information, not repeat the subject.

Good subject: "The metric that predicted 72% of our churned accounts"
Good preview: "Plus: 3 dashboard templates you can steal today."

Bad subject: "This week's newsletter — March edition!"
Bad preview: "The metric that predicted 72% of our churned accounts" (repeating the subject)

**Primary Content Section:** This is the reason people subscribed. It must deliver value that they cannot easily find elsewhere. Start with a hook — a surprising data point, a contrarian opinion, or a specific story. Write in first person. Share a perspective, not just information.

Good: "Last month, I spent $2,400 on a marketing dashboard that showed us exactly one useful number. Here's what I learned about the difference between data you can see and data you can act on."

Bad: "Marketing dashboards are important for tracking metrics. In this section, we'll discuss different types of dashboards."

**Curated Content:** Curation adds value when you add your take. A bare list of links is an RSS feed, not a newsletter. For each curated item, include a 1-sentence description that tells the reader why it matters and what they will get from clicking.

Good: "How Notion's marketing team builds landing pages in 2 hours (Process Street) — Their template system is worth stealing. They use a 6-section framework that starts with the objection, not the feature."

Bad: "Interesting article about landing pages: [link]"

**Promotional Section:** If you include a promotional section, keep it under 20% of the newsletter. Frame it as value-first — what does the reader get? — not company-first. If the newsletter is consistently valuable, readers will tolerate a soft promo. If the newsletter is a thinly disguised ad, they will unsubscribe.

**Footer:** The closing line is a human moment. Sign off personally. Include a forwarding CTA — "Know someone who would find this useful? Forward this email." Referral programs go here if applicable.

### Conventions

- One theme per issue. Every section should connect to the theme, even loosely.
- Subject lines are 40-60 characters. Under 40 can feel incomplete. Over 60 gets truncated on mobile.
- Preview text is 40-90 characters and adds new information — never repeats the subject line.
- Primary content section is 150-400 words. This is the anchor — long enough to be valuable, short enough to be read in 3 minutes.
- Total newsletter length is under 800 words. Newsletters that take more than 5 minutes to read get saved for later and never opened.
- Promotional content is 20% or less of the total issue. Readers subscribed for value, not ads.
- Send time is consistent. Same day, same time, every issue. Consistency builds habit.
- From name is a person, not a brand. "Alex from Meridian" outperforms "Meridian Analytics" for open rates.
- Every curated link includes a 1-sentence editorial take. Bare links are not curation.
- Reply-to goes to a monitored inbox. Newsletter replies are gold — product feedback, content ideas, relationship builders.
- Include a forwarding or referral CTA in every issue. Word-of-mouth is the highest-quality growth channel for newsletters.
- Plain text formatting is preferred over heavy HTML. Newsletter emails that look like web pages get filtered more often.

### Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT send newsletters without a consistent schedule. Subscribers expect regularity. Random sends erode trust and tank open rates.
- Do NOT make every newsletter about your product. If the newsletter is just a product update disguised as content, readers will tune out.
- Do NOT use generic subject lines like "Monthly Newsletter" or "Weekly Digest #47." These give the reader zero reason to open.
- Do NOT repeat the subject line in the preview text. This wastes the most valuable inbox real estate you have after the subject.
- Do NOT write newsletters in corporate third person. "The team is excited to announce" is not how people write emails. Write in first person.
- Do NOT include more than 5 links in a single issue. Too many choices reduce clicks on all of them. Focus on the links that matter.
- Do NOT skip the closing line. A newsletter that ends abruptly after the last link feels transactional. A personal sign-off builds relationship.
- Do NOT send without proofreading in the actual email client. Formatting that looks correct in the editor often breaks in Gmail, Outlook, or Apple Mail. Send a test email first.
- Do NOT use "no-reply" as the from address. Newsletters are conversations, not broadcasts.
- Do NOT bury the best content at the bottom. Lead with your strongest section. Most readers read the first section and skim the rest.
- Do NOT forget mobile optimization. Over 60% of email opens happen on mobile. Use single-column layouts, readable font sizes (16px+), and tappable links.
- Do NOT include an image-heavy header that pushes the first sentence below the fold on mobile. The first line of content should be visible without scrolling.
- Do NOT change your format every issue. Readers build reading habits around consistent structure. If your newsletter always starts with a personal insight, do not randomly start with a curated link list.
- Do NOT send a newsletter just because it is Tuesday. If you do not have something valuable to say, skip the issue. A late issue is better than a bad issue.

**Type:** Original analysis

```
Last quarter, we lost a $180K deal that our pipeline dashboard said was "on track."

The lead score was 92. The champion was engaged. The demo went well. Every metric we tracked said this deal would close.

It didn't. And when I dug into why, I found the problem wasn't in the data we were looking at — it was in the data we weren't.

Our pipeline dashboard tracked deal count, deal value, and stage progression. What it didn't track was pipeline velocity — how fast deals move through each stage.

This deal sat in "proposal sent" for 47 days. Our average for that stage is 11 days. If we had been tracking velocity, we would have flagged it at day 20 and intervened. Instead, we watched the deal value sit in our forecast and felt good about our quarter.

After that loss, we added pipeline velocity to every dashboard. Here's what we found:

- Deals that exceed 2x the average stage duration close at 18% the rate of on-track deals
- Pipeline velocity predicted 72% of our lost deals in the following quarter — better than lead score, engagement score, or champion strength
- The insight is not "slow deals are bad" (obvious). It's that velocity is a leading indicator while most pipeline metrics are lagging.

The formula is simple:

Pipeline Velocity = (# of Deals × Average Deal Value × Win Rate) / Average Sales Cycle Length

Track this number monthly. When it drops, you know 60-90 days before your revenue does.

I wrote a deeper breakdown on our blog — including the 4 dashboard changes we made and the Looker template we use.
```

### Customization

Adjust the number of sections based on your format. A "one big idea" newsletter has one primary content section and a brief footer — no curated links, no promotions. A "digest" format has 5-7 short curated sections with brief commentary. For product-centric newsletters (changelog, updates), replace the curated section with a feature spotlight and customer tip. For paid newsletters, remove promotional sections entirely and increase primary content depth to 600-800 words. Match the format to your audience's expectation and your content velocity.
