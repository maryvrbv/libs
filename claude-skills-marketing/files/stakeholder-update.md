## Skill: Marketing Stakeholder Update

A marketing stakeholder update is the regular performance report a marketing lead sends to executives, cross-functional partners, and leadership. It answers three questions fast: how are campaigns performing, where is the budget going, and what needs attention. Most stakeholders spend less than two minutes reading these. Every word has to earn its place.

### How to Write Each Section

**Status Indicator**
This is the first thing leadership sees. Be honest. "On Track" means campaigns are hitting targets, budget is on plan, and no blockers exist that threaten the quarter. "At Risk" means a key metric is trending wrong, a major campaign underperformed, or a dependency (creative, budget approval, vendor) is unresolved. "Blocked" means marketing output has stopped — waiting on legal review, budget freeze, or a critical tool outage.

Good: `At Risk` — because paid search CPA increased 40% after a competitor entered the auction and the current budget cannot sustain target volume.
Bad: `On Track` — while CPA has doubled and you are quietly reallocating budget hoping nobody notices.

**TL;DR**
Write this last. It is the executive summary — 2-3 sentences maximum. Lead with the most important number (revenue attributed, pipeline generated, leads acquired). Follow with the most significant change (campaign win, channel shift, budget issue). Close with what you need or what is coming next. The TL;DR should be self-contained.

Good: "Marketing-sourced pipeline reached $1.2M this period, up 18% from last month. The product launch campaign drove 340 qualified leads at $42 CPL, beating the $55 target. We need budget approval for a $15K influencer campaign by Friday to hold the Q2 launch timeline."
Bad: "The team has been busy across multiple channels and we're seeing some positive trends in several areas."

**Key Metrics**
Pick 4-6 metrics that represent the full marketing picture. Always include a revenue or pipeline metric, an acquisition metric, an engagement metric, and an efficiency metric. Show current value, previous period value, percentage change, target, and whether the metric is on or off track. Keep the same metrics every update so stakeholders can track trends.

Good metrics to include:
- Revenue attributed to marketing (or marketing-sourced pipeline)
- Marketing Qualified Leads (MQLs) or Sales Qualified Leads (SQLs)
- Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) or Cost Per Lead (CPL)
- Website traffic or conversion rate
- Email list growth or engagement rate

Bad metrics: Total impressions (vanity), social followers (vanity without engagement context), "content pieces published" (output, not outcome).

**Campaign Highlights**
Split into active campaigns, top performers, and underperformers. The campaign table gives a quick status snapshot. Top performers explain what worked and why — this builds institutional knowledge. Underperformers explain what missed and what you are doing about it. Never hide underperformers. Leadership respects honesty and will find out anyway.

Good top performer: "The 'Ship Your First App' webinar drove 280 registrations (2.1x target) at $12 CPL. LinkedIn ads outperformed Meta by 3x on qualified lead rate. Scaling LinkedIn spend next period."
Bad: "The webinar went well."

Good underperformer: "Google Search campaign for 'AI design tools' — CPC increased 65% after two new competitors entered the auction. Paused broad match keywords, shifting budget to long-tail terms with stronger intent. Expect CPA to normalize within 2 weeks."
Bad: "Some campaigns didn't do as well as hoped."

**Channel Performance**
Show every active channel in one table. Include reach/traffic, conversions, conversion rate, spend (if paid), and ROAS or ROI. Add a trend indicator so leadership can see which channels are improving and which are declining. This table makes portfolio-level decisions possible — if organic search is growing while paid social is declining, it surfaces the reallocation conversation.

**Budget vs. Actual**
Show budget categories, planned spend, actual spend, and variance. If variance exceeds 10%, add a note explaining why. This section builds trust with finance. If you are consistently under budget, leadership will question your forecasting. If you are consistently over, they will question your discipline. Aim for variance under 5%.

**Risks & Blockers**
Be specific about impact. "Creative resources are tight" is vague. "The design team has no capacity until April 3 — the Q2 campaign creative will be delayed by 10 days, pushing the paid launch past the conference window" is actionable. Every risk needs an owner and a status.

**Asks**
Be explicit. State what you need, from whom, and by when. If you need budget approval, state the exact amount. If you need a decision, frame the options. If you need resources, specify the role and duration. Make it easy for the decision-maker to say yes or no in one meeting.

**What's Next**
2-3 bullets on the plan for the next reporting period. Each bullet should answer: "What will be different by the next update?" Be specific about campaigns launching, experiments running, or milestones targeted.

### Conventions

- Length: The entire update should fit on one screen (40-70 lines of markdown). If it is longer, cut. Stakeholders will not read a marketing novel.
- Cadence: State whether this is weekly, biweekly, or monthly. Adjust detail accordingly — weekly updates are tighter; monthly updates include more trend analysis and budget detail.
- Metrics consistency: Use the same metrics every update. If you change a metric, add a footnote explaining why.
- Currency: Always include the currency symbol ($, EUR, etc.) with financial figures. Round to meaningful precision — $12,847 is fine, $12,847.23 is noise.
- Percentages: Show percentage change, not just raw numbers. "CPA is $45" means nothing without knowing it was $38 last period.
- Attribution: When a campaign wins, credit the team and channel. When something misses, name the cause and the owner of the fix.
- Trend arrows: Use consistent direction indicators (up/down/flat or percentage) across all tables.
- Links: Link to dashboards, campaign briefs, or supporting docs rather than inlining data. Keep the update lean.
- Dates: Use YYYY-MM-DD format in all tables. Relative dates are acceptable in prose.
- Versioning: Include the date in the subject line and keep a running archive so leadership can track quarter-over-quarter trends.
- ROAS: Always show Return on Ad Spend as a ratio (e.g., 3.2x means $3.20 returned per $1 spent). Define it once if your audience is not familiar with the term.
- Definitions: If using acronyms (MQL, SQL, CAC, CPL, ROAS), define them in the first update and assume familiarity in subsequent ones.

### Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT bury bad news. If a campaign missed its target by 40%, that belongs in the TL;DR, not buried in paragraph 6.
- Do NOT report impressions as a primary metric. Impressions without engagement or conversion context are vanity numbers that erode credibility.
- Do NOT skip the budget section. Finance reads these updates. If you do not show budget discipline, they will impose it on you.
- Do NOT write "making good progress" or "seeing positive trends" without numbers. Quantify or do not say it.
- Do NOT include more than 6 key metrics. A dashboard with 15 metrics tells leadership nothing.
- Do NOT mix timeframes. If the update covers March 1-15, every metric and campaign result should cover March 1-15. Do not mix weekly, monthly, and quarterly numbers in the same table.
- Do NOT report channel performance without conversion rates. Traffic without conversion is a cost, not a result.
- Do NOT default to "On Track" when CPA is rising, pipeline is flat, and you have no plan. At Risk is not a failure — it is responsible communication.
- Do NOT list activities instead of outcomes. "Published 12 blog posts" is not a result. "Blog content drove 840 organic visits and 23 MQLs" is.
- Do NOT forget to include ROAS or ROI on paid channels. Spend without return context is incomplete.
- Do NOT include raw data dumps. Summarize. The dashboard link is for people who want to drill down.
- Do NOT write an update without an ask or an explicit "No asks this cycle." Ambiguity about what you need is worse than asking for nothing.
- Do NOT compare metrics to targets without showing the trend. A metric that is off-target but improving tells a different story than one that is off-target and declining.
- Do NOT use different formats each time. Consistency builds trust. Stakeholders should know exactly where to look for the information they care about.
- Do NOT omit underperformers. Cherry-picking wins while hiding losses destroys credibility the moment someone checks the dashboard themselves.

### Adapting for Different Audiences

**C-suite / Board:** Lead with revenue impact and pipeline metrics. Minimize channel-level detail. Emphasize CAC trends, ROAS, and budget efficiency. Keep it under 40 lines. The TL;DR should read like a business update, not a marketing update.

**Cross-functional partners (Sales, Product, CS):** Emphasize lead quality, handoff metrics, and campaign-to-pipeline attribution. Sales cares about MQL-to-SQL conversion rate and lead source quality, not your LinkedIn CTR. Product cares about positioning feedback and competitive intelligence from campaigns.

**Marketing team:** Include more channel and creative detail. Show what is working at the tactic level — ad copy variants, landing page performance, audience segments. This is where the team learns and iterates.

**Weekly cadence:** Focus on what changed this week, any new risks, and the plan for next week. The metrics table shows week-over-week changes. Keep it under 40 lines.

**Monthly cadence:** Include more trend analysis. Add a "Trends" section below the metrics table that calls out multi-week patterns. Include budget year-to-date tracking. These can run up to 80 lines.
