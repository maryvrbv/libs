# Case Study

A customer success story framework that uses measurable results to prove product value to prospective buyers. The marketer uses this when a customer has achieved significant, quantifiable outcomes and agreed to be featured — turning their success into a persuasion tool for sales conversations, landing pages, and content marketing.

## Before you start

- **Product context.** This framework needs to know the product: name, what it does, who it serves. Take it from the request, the project's CLAUDE.md or README, or earlier conversation. If it is missing and matters for the output, ask one short question instead of inventing facts.
- **Language.** The framework is written in English; write the deliverable in the language of the request (Russian request → Russian output), and adapt examples, platforms and conventions to that market.
- **No invented numbers.** Placeholders in `[brackets]` are to be filled with real data from the user. Where data is missing, leave a clearly marked placeholder rather than fabricating metrics, quotes or customer names.

## How to Write Each Section

**Headline & Summary:** The headline must include the customer name, the product name, and a specific result with a number. This is not a place for cleverness — it is a place for proof. The key results table at the top lets busy readers get the outcome in 5 seconds.

Good: "How Meridian Logistics Reduced Order Processing Time by 62% with ShipStack"
Bad: "Meridian Logistics Transforms Their Operations with Our Amazing Platform"

**Customer Context:** Give enough background for the reader to think "that sounds like my company." Include industry, size, and relevant details that make the customer relatable to your target buyer. Do not write the customer's entire company history.

Good: "Meridian Logistics is a mid-market 3PL provider handling 4,200 orders per day across 3 warehouses in the Pacific Northwest. They serve D2C e-commerce brands with 2-day delivery guarantees."

Bad: "Meridian Logistics is a leading logistics company that was founded in 2012 and has grown to become a trusted partner for many businesses."

**Challenge:** Name the specific pain with quantified business impact. "Order processing was slow" is not a challenge. "Each order took 14 minutes to process manually, and during peak season the team fell 800 orders behind per day" is a challenge. Include what the customer was doing before — this establishes the baseline for the results section.

**Solution:** Explain why they chose your product over alternatives (this handles the "why not competitor X" objection for future prospects). The implementation timeline shows that adoption is realistic. The features table connects product capabilities to specific use cases.

**Results:** Every result must be quantified with a before/after comparison and a measurement method. "Improved efficiency" is not a result. "Reduced order processing time from 14 minutes to 5.3 minutes (62% reduction), measured by average processing time in ShipStack analytics over 90 days" is a result. Include a timeline to results so prospects can set realistic expectations.

**Customer Quotes:** Use real quotes from real people with their real titles. Each quote should appear in the section it supports — a quote about the challenge goes in the Challenge section, not the Results section. Quotes add credibility that your prose cannot.

## Conventions

- Every case study includes at least 3 quantified results with before/after numbers.
- Customer quotes appear in at least 3 sections: Challenge, Solution, and Results.
- Quotes are attributed with full name, title, and company. Anonymous quotes carry no weight.
- The headline includes the customer name and a specific numeric result. No vague headlines.
- Implementation timelines are specific. "A few weeks" is not a timeline. "3 weeks from kickoff to full deployment" is.
- The customer profile table provides enough detail for a prospect to say "that sounds like us."
- Results include measurement methodology. How you measured matters as much as what you measured.
- The case study reads as a narrative: Context → Problem → Solution → Proof → Next Steps.
- Total length is 800-1,500 words. Long enough to be credible, short enough to be read.
- Customer approval is required before publication. Document approval status in the metadata.
- The CTA at the end matches the buyer's likely next step. High-intent readers get "Book a demo." Early-stage readers get "See pricing" or "Start free trial."

## Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT write a case study without quantified results. If the customer cannot provide numbers, it is a testimonial, not a case study.
- Do NOT use vague language instead of specific metrics. "Significant improvement" and "much faster" are meaningless without numbers.
- Do NOT skip the "before" numbers. Results without a baseline are unverifiable. "Processed 4,200 orders per day" means nothing without knowing the previous rate.
- Do NOT write the challenge section from your perspective. Write it from the customer's perspective using their language. The pain should feel real and specific.
- Do NOT list features without connecting them to outcomes. "They use our AI-powered analytics dashboard" does not help the reader. "They use the analytics dashboard to identify delayed shipments in real-time, which reduced missed delivery SLAs from 12% to 2.1%" does.
- Do NOT attribute quotes to "a customer" or "the team." Every quote needs a name and title. Anonymous quotes read as fabricated.
- Do NOT make the case study about your product. The customer is the protagonist, not you. The narrative is about their problem and their success — your product is the tool that enabled it.
- Do NOT include results you cannot verify. If the customer said "we saved about $500K" but cannot substantiate it, do not include the number. One unverifiable claim discredits the entire case study.
- Do NOT forget the implementation timeline. Prospects need to know how long it takes to see results. Omitting this creates unrealistic expectations or fear that it will take forever.
- Do NOT end without a CTA. The reader just consumed proof that your product works. Tell them what to do next.
- Do NOT publish without customer sign-off. Legal, brand, and factual review by the customer is non-negotiable.
- Do NOT write case studies that only appeal to one persona. Include details that resonate with the decision maker (ROI, strategic impact), the user (daily workflow improvements), and the evaluator (implementation details, integrations).

## 2. Customer Context

**Company Overview:** Meridian Logistics is a 3PL provider based in Portland, Oregon, operating 3 warehouses across the Pacific Northwest. They process an average of 4,200 orders per day for 34 D2C e-commerce brands, with a 2-day delivery guarantee for 85% of the continental US.

**Relevant Context:** The D2C fulfillment market is consolidating around speed. Meridian's clients were threatening to move to competitors offering same-day processing guarantees. At the same time, Meridian's warehouse team had grown from 120 to 210 fulfillment workers in 18 months, and their legacy order management system could not scale with the team.

## 3. Challenge

**Problem Statement:** Meridian's legacy order management system required fulfillment coordinators to manually route each order through a 7-step process: intake, verification, inventory check, pick assignment, pack instruction, shipping label generation, and tracking update. Each step required switching between 3 different systems — their WMS, the carrier portal, and a custom spreadsheet tracker.

### Specific Challenges

| Challenge | Impact | Previous Approach |

**Customer Quote (Challenge):**
> "Our coordinators were spending 80% of their day copying data between systems. During peak season, we had 800 orders sitting unprocessed at the end of every shift. Two of our largest clients put us on performance improvement plans."
> — Rachel Osei, VP Operations, Meridian Logistics

## 4. Solution

**Why They Chose ShipStack:** Meridian evaluated ShipStack, ShipBob's 3PL tools, and a custom-built solution. They chose ShipStack because it integrated natively with their existing WMS (Manhattan Associates) and all 4 of their carrier partners (UPS, FedEx, USPS, OnTrac) without requiring a WMS migration. The 3-week implementation timeline (vs. 4 months for the custom build) was the deciding factor.

| Setup | ShipStack team connected WMS and carrier APIs, configured routing rules, trained operations managers | 2 weeks |
| Pilot | 1 warehouse (Portland) processed all orders through ShipStack. Other 2 warehouses on legacy system. | 2 weeks |
| Full Rollout | All 3 warehouses on ShipStack. Legacy system decommissioned. | 1 week |

### Key Features Used

| Feature | How They Use It | Impact |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| Automated Order Routing | Orders auto-route through intake → pick → pack → ship based on rules (product type, carrier, destination) | Eliminated 5 of 7 manual steps per order |

## Customization

Adjust the depth based on the customer's story and your audience. For enterprise sales, expand the Implementation section with more detail about integrations, change management, and security review. For product-led growth, trim the Implementation section and emphasize speed to value. If the customer provided limited data, focus on fewer metrics with deeper context rather than padding with vague numbers. For video case studies, use this structure as the script outline and add a shot list column to the tables.
