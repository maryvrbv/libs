# Landing Page Copy

A complete landing page copywriting framework that converts visitors into signups, trials, or purchases. The marketer uses this when building a new landing page or rewriting an existing one — whether for a product launch, a campaign, or a feature page that needs to drive a specific action.

## Before you start

- **Product context.** This framework needs to know the product: name, what it does, who it serves. Take it from the request, the project's CLAUDE.md or README, or earlier conversation. If it is missing and matters for the output, ask one short question instead of inventing facts.
- **Language.** The framework is written in English; write the deliverable in the language of the request (Russian request → Russian output), and adapt examples, platforms and conventions to that market.
- **No invented numbers.** Placeholders in `[brackets]` are to be filled with real data from the user. Where data is missing, leave a clearly marked placeholder rather than fabricating metrics, quotes or customer names.

## How to Write Each Section

**Messaging Strategy:** Define this before writing a single word of copy. The core value proposition is the backbone — every section on the page must support it. The awareness level determines how much education the page needs. A Product-Aware visitor needs less context than a Problem-Aware one.

Good: "Core value proposition: Ship projects 3x faster with AI-powered project management that writes your status updates, flags risks, and keeps stakeholders aligned automatically."

Bad: "Core value proposition: We're a project management platform."

**Hero Section:** The headline is the most important line on the page. It must state the outcome the visitor wants — not what the product is. The subheadline adds the "how." The CTA button tells them what to do. The supporting text removes the last objection.

Good headline: "Ship projects on time without the status meeting chaos"
Bad headline: "The AI-Powered Project Management Platform for Modern Teams"

**Pain Points:** Name the pain specifically enough that the visitor thinks "that's exactly my problem." Vague pain ("managing projects is hard") does not resonate. Specific pain ("You spend 6 hours a week writing status updates nobody reads") does.

**Benefits Section:** Benefits are not features. A feature is what the product does. A benefit is what changes in the user's life. Write benefits as outcomes, not capabilities.

Good: "Get 6 hours back every week — automated status reports pull data from your tools and write themselves."
Bad: "AI-powered status report generation feature."

**Social Proof:** Testimonials must include a specific result with a number. "Great product, love it!" is not social proof. "We reduced project delays by 40% in the first quarter" is. Include the customer's real name, title, and company.

**FAQ Section:** Every FAQ should address an objection. "How does pricing work?" is a real question. "What makes you different from [competitor]?" is an objection. Answer directly without hedging.

## Conventions

- The hero headline is the first thing a visitor reads. Make it about their outcome, not your product.
- One primary CTA per page. It can appear multiple times (hero, mid-page, bottom), but it is always the same action.
- CTA buttons use action verbs: "Start free trial," "Get started," "Book a demo." Never "Submit" or "Learn more."
- Social proof appears within the first 2 scroll depths. Do not bury it at the bottom.
- Every testimonial includes a real name, job title, company, and a measurable result.
- Pain points come before benefits. Establish the problem before presenting the solution.
- Feature descriptions are written in user terms, not technical terms. "Automated reporting" not "ML-driven analytics pipeline."
- The page reads top-to-bottom as a complete argument. Hero (promise) → Pain (problem) → Benefits (solution) → Proof (evidence) → Features (details) → CTA (action).
- Mobile layout is considered during writing. Headlines under 8 words. Paragraphs under 3 sentences.
- Pricing section only appears if this is a pricing-specific page or the product has simple, public pricing. Complex pricing gets a "Talk to sales" CTA instead.
- FAQ answers are 2-3 sentences maximum. If the answer needs a paragraph, it belongs on a separate page.

## Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT write headlines that describe the product category instead of the outcome. "Project Management Software" is a category. "Ship every project on time" is an outcome.
- Do NOT use more than one primary CTA on a page. "Start trial" and "Book demo" and "Download guide" creates decision paralysis.
- Do NOT bury social proof below the fold. Visitors need trust signals early — put a logo bar or user count in the hero.
- Do NOT write testimonials without specific results. "Amazing product!" does not move anyone. A number and a timeframe does.
- Do NOT list features without explaining the benefit. "Real-time Gantt charts" means nothing to someone who does not use Gantt charts. "See every deadline and dependency at a glance" is the benefit.
- Do NOT skip the pain points section. If you go straight to "here's what we do," the visitor has no reason to care.
- Do NOT use jargon the visitor does not use. Write in the language your customers use in support tickets and sales calls, not the language your engineering team uses.
- Do NOT make the FAQ section an afterthought. It is where you handle objections that the rest of the page missed.
- Do NOT use "we" more than "you." The page is about the visitor, not the company.
- Do NOT forget the final CTA section. A visitor who scrolls to the bottom of the page is interested — give them a clear next step.
- Do NOT use vague supporting text under CTAs. "Get started today" says nothing. "Free 14-day trial. No credit card required." removes friction.
- Do NOT write a 3,000-word landing page when 800 words will do. Length depends on price and commitment level. Free trial? Short page. $10K/year contract? Long page.
- Do NOT ignore the traffic source. Visitors from a Google ad need different context than visitors from an email campaign. Adjust the hero messaging to match the ad or email that brought them.

## Customization

Adjust section depth based on your product's price point and complexity. A $9/month tool needs a shorter page — hero, 3 benefits, social proof, CTA. A $50K/year enterprise product needs every section plus a case study section and a security/compliance section. For campaign-specific landing pages (ad traffic for a specific feature), strip out the pricing and FAQ sections and focus the entire page on one benefit with a single CTA. For product-led growth (free trial conversion), emphasize the "How It Works" section and make the CTA about getting started, not talking to sales.
