# Marketing Brief

A marketing brief is the context document that brings a new team member, agency, or freelancer up to speed on your brand, audience, competitive landscape, and active campaigns. A marketer writes this when onboarding a new agency partner, hiring a freelance writer or designer, or bringing a new marketing hire onto the team. It eliminates the weeks of "figuring things out" that kill momentum.

## Before you start

- **Product context.** This framework needs to know the product: name, what it does, who it serves. Take it from the request, the project's CLAUDE.md or README, or earlier conversation. If it is missing and matters for the output, ask one short question instead of inventing facts.
- **Language.** The framework is written in English; write the deliverable in the language of the request (Russian request → Russian output), and adapt examples, platforms and conventions to that market.
- **No invented numbers.** Placeholders in `[brackets]` are to be filled with real data from the user. Where data is missing, leave a clearly marked placeholder rather than fabricating metrics, quotes or customer names.

## How to Write Each Section

**Brand Overview**
This is the foundation. Write it for someone who has never heard of the company. The "What We Do" section should be a plain-language explanation that a non-industry person can understand. The "How We're Different" section should name the specific positioning — not "we're the best" but the actual strategic distinction. The key messages table should contain the exact phrases the team uses — these are the approved messages, not guidelines for creating new ones.

Good "What We Do": "Nexus Analytics is a product analytics platform that helps SaaS product teams understand how users interact with their software. Teams use Nexus to track feature adoption, identify drop-off points, and make data-driven product decisions."
Bad: "Nexus is a next-generation analytics solution that leverages cutting-edge technology to deliver actionable insights and drive business value."

Good "How We're Different": "Nexus is the only product analytics tool designed for teams without a data analyst. Setup takes 15 minutes (vs. weeks for Amplitude or Mixpanel), and every report includes a plain-language summary explaining what the data means and what to do about it."
Bad: "We're better, faster, and cheaper than the competition."

**Audience Profiles**
Build profiles that are specific enough to write to. Include pain points in the audience's own language (pull from customer interviews, support tickets, or sales call notes). The "Where They Spend Time" field directly informs channel strategy. The "How They Evaluate Solutions" field informs the content funnel. The "Common Objections" field informs messaging.

Good pain point: "Spends 3+ hours per week manually compiling analytics reports from multiple tools — time they would rather spend on product decisions."
Bad pain point: "Needs better analytics."

**Competitive Landscape**
Be honest about competitor strengths. An agency or freelancer who discovers that a competitor is stronger than you indicated will lose trust in the brief. The "Our Advantage" column should be specific and defensible. The "Rules of Engagement" section prevents the common mistake of a new partner writing aggressive competitive content that does not match the brand voice.

**Current Campaigns & Initiatives**
This is the situational awareness section. A new partner needs to know what is already running so they do not duplicate effort or contradict existing messaging. Include upcoming launches so they can plan ahead. The content strategy summary gives them the editorial context.

**Tools & Access**
List every tool the person will need, the access level required, and how to get it. This section prevents the common delay pattern where a new partner cannot start work because they are waiting for access to the analytics dashboard or the ad platform.

**Working With Us**
Set expectations for communication, approvals, and escalation. This prevents friction. If the approval process takes 5 business days, say so upfront — do not let a freelancer discover it when they miss a deadline because they submitted for review too late.

## Conventions

- Write the brief before the onboarding call, not during it. The call should be a discussion of the brief, not the creation of it.
- Keep the brief under 5 pages (roughly 200-300 lines of markdown). Comprehensive but scannable. Link to deeper resources rather than including everything.
- Update the brief quarterly or whenever something significant changes (new product, repositioning, new competitor, org changes).
- Mark the classification clearly. If the brief contains revenue numbers, competitive intelligence, or strategic plans, it should be marked confidential.
- Include real numbers wherever possible. "Our CAC is $142" builds trust. "Our CAC is competitive" does not.
- Use the audience's language in the pain points section, not your internal language. Quote customer interviews if you have them.
- The competitive landscape should be factual, not promotional. An honest assessment helps partners write better content.
- Include the approval process and turnaround times. The #1 cause of agency/freelancer frustration is unclear approval workflows.
- Name specific contacts with their responsibilities. "Ask marketing" is not helpful. "Ask Sarah Kim (Content Lead) for editorial questions, Marcus Webb (Demand Gen) for paid campaign questions" is.
- Brand guidelines should include links to actual assets, not descriptions. "Our primary color is blue" is less useful than "Primary: #2563EB. Brand kit: [link]."
- Tools and access should be set up before the partner's first day, not after.
- If the brief is for a specific engagement (e.g., "write 4 blog posts"), include the scope and deliverables at the top.

## Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT write a brief that reads like a press release. This is an internal document — be honest about challenges, gaps, and weaknesses.
- Do NOT skip the competitive section. Every new partner's first question will be "who are we up against?" If you do not answer it, they will Google it and form their own (possibly wrong) impression.
- Do NOT include aspirational positioning that does not match reality. If the product is early-stage with 50 customers, do not describe it as "the leading platform." The partner will see through it immediately.
- Do NOT leave the audience profiles generic. "Marketing professionals aged 25-55" is useless. The more specific the profile, the better the work product.
- Do NOT forget the "Working With Us" section. Process misalignment causes more agency/freelancer churn than quality issues.
- Do NOT list tools without access instructions. A brief that says "we use HubSpot" without explaining how to get access is incomplete.
- Do NOT write key messages that are internally focused. "We are a Series B startup disrupting the analytics space" is how you talk to investors, not how you talk to customers. Key messages should be customer-facing.
- Do NOT include every metric you track. Include only the metrics the partner needs to understand performance. A freelance writer does not need to know your burn rate.
- Do NOT skip the content strategy summary. Without editorial context, a new writer will produce content that does not fit your existing program.
- Do NOT assume the partner knows your industry. Write for a smart generalist, not a domain expert.
- Do NOT make the brief a static document that sits in a folder. Share it, review it together, and update it when things change.
- Do NOT include sensitive financial data (revenue, runway, investor details) unless the partner has signed an NDA and needs the information.
- Do NOT write the brief as a wish list. It should reflect current reality, not where you hope to be in 6 months.

### Indirect Competitors / Alternatives
- **Spreadsheets + Notion** — many teams manually track feedback in spreadsheets or Notion databases. Works until the volume exceeds what one person can manage (typically ~100 feedback items/month).

### Competitive Rules of Engagement
We are factual and fair when discussing competitors. In blog content, we name competitors directly in comparison guides but never disparage them — we focus on what makes Driftwood different, not what makes them bad. In social media, we do not engage with competitor posts or start comparison threads. In sales enablement, we provide honest strengths and weaknesses for each competitor.

## Customization

Replace all bracket sections with your actual brand, team, and campaign details. Adjust the audience profiles to match your real personas — use actual customer language from interviews or support conversations. The competitive landscape should reflect your honest assessment. The tools section should list every tool the partner will need access to.

Update this brief quarterly. When campaigns change, competitors shift, or the team evolves, the brief should reflect current reality. A stale brief is worse than no brief — it creates false confidence.

For different partner types, adjust the depth:
- **Content agency:** Emphasize audience profiles, brand voice, content strategy, and editorial process.
- **Paid media agency:** Emphasize audience profiles, channel strategy, metrics, and budget.
- **Freelance designer:** Emphasize brand guidelines, design assets, and approval process.
- **New marketing hire:** Include everything — this is their onboarding manual.
