# Content Roadmap Presentation

A content roadmap presentation is the quarterly plan that a content or marketing leader presents to stakeholders. It translates content strategy into a concrete plan with themes, timelines, resource requirements, and success metrics. A marketer writes this at the end of each quarter to align the team and secure buy-in for the next quarter's content investment.

## Before you start

- **Product context.** This framework needs to know the product: name, what it does, who it serves. Take it from the request, the project's CLAUDE.md or README, or earlier conversation. If it is missing and matters for the output, ask one short question instead of inventing facts.
- **Language.** The framework is written in English; write the deliverable in the language of the request (Russian request → Russian output), and adapt examples, platforms and conventions to that market.
- **No invented numbers.** Placeholders in `[brackets]` are to be filled with real data from the user. Where data is missing, leave a clearly marked placeholder rather than fabricating metrics, quotes or customer names.

## How to Write Each Section

**Content Vision**
This is the strategic frame — not a mission statement, but a concrete description of what the content program will accomplish this quarter and why. It should connect to at least one business goal (growth, retention, positioning, market expansion). Write it last, after you have built the plan, so it accurately reflects the work.

Good: "In Q3, our content shifts from awareness to conversion. Last quarter established thought leadership in the AI scheduling space with 12 published pieces driving 34K monthly visits. This quarter, we build the middle and bottom of the funnel — comparison guides, ROI calculators, and customer stories that move prospects from interest to trial. The goal: increase content-attributed trial starts from 120/month to 200/month."
Bad: "We will continue to create high-quality content that resonates with our audience."

**Last Quarter in Review**
Anchor this quarter's plan in what you learned last quarter. Show 4-5 metrics with goals vs. actuals. Then list 3 key learnings — what worked and will carry forward, what did not work and will change, and any new insight that shapes this quarter's plan. This section shows stakeholders that the plan is evidence-based, not aspirational.

Good learning: "Long-form comparison guides drove 3x the trial signups of general thought leadership posts, despite getting 40% less traffic. Shifting 30% of content effort from awareness content to comparison content this quarter."
Bad learning: "Blog is doing well."

**Content Themes & Pillars**
Organize the quarter's content into 2-4 themes. Each theme should map to a business objective or audience need. Under each theme, list the specific content pieces with format, channel, target date, and owner. The "Why this theme" explanation is critical — it connects each piece of content to the strategic rationale.

Limit to 3-4 themes. More than that means you are spreading too thin. Each theme should have 3-8 content pieces across the quarter. If a theme has only 1 piece, it is not a theme — it is a one-off.

**Content Calendar Overview**
A week-by-week view of what publishes when. This is the operational plan. Keep it to one table per month. Include format and channel so stakeholders understand the publishing cadence. This is not the detailed production calendar — it is the summary view for alignment.

**Distribution Plan**
Publishing is half the job. This section shows how each piece of content reaches the audience. List every distribution channel, the content types that go through it, the frequency, the primary metric, and the owner. Paid promotion budget goes here if you are boosting content.

**Resource Requirements**
Be explicit about what the plan requires — people hours, tools, and budget. If there is a gap between current capacity and what the plan demands, name the gap and the plan to fill it. This is where you make the case for headcount, contractors, or tool investment. Without this section, stakeholders approve the plan without understanding the cost, and you end up under-resourced.

**Success Metrics**
Define 4-6 metrics with current baselines and quarterly targets. Include how you will measure each one — the tool, the dashboard, the attribution model. This is the contract between the content team and leadership: "We will deliver this, measured this way."

**Risks**
Name anything that could derail the plan: resource constraints, competitive moves, algorithm changes, key person dependency, seasonal factors. For each risk, state the probability, the impact, and the mitigation plan. This section shows strategic maturity — you have thought about what could go wrong and have a plan.

## Conventions

- Present the roadmap quarterly. Monthly planning is too granular for stakeholders; annual planning is too vague to be actionable.
- Limit to 3-4 themes. If the plan has 8 themes, it has no focus.
- Every content piece in the calendar should trace back to a theme. Orphan content without strategic rationale dilutes the plan.
- Include the prior quarter's results. Plans without historical context are guesswork.
- Resource requirements must be realistic. If you have 1 writer and the plan requires 12 pieces/month, stakeholders need to know before they approve.
- Budget should match the ambition. If the plan calls for video content but the budget has zero video production line items, the plan is fiction.
- Metrics should be measurable with tools you actually have access to. Do not promise content-attributed revenue if you do not have multi-touch attribution set up.
- Distribution is not optional. For every piece of content, show how it reaches the audience. "Build it and they will come" is not a strategy.
- Name owners for every content piece and every channel. Unnamed work does not get done.
- Format the document for presentation — use tables, keep prose minimal, and structure for scanning. This will likely be presented in a meeting, not read as a memo.
- Include a "last quarter" section even if results were poor. Honest assessment builds credibility.
- Update the calendar monthly as pieces move from planned to published. The roadmap is a living document.

## Anti-Patterns

- Do NOT present a content roadmap without metrics. If you cannot measure success, you cannot justify the investment.
- Do NOT list content ideas without publication dates. A list of topics is a brainstorm, not a roadmap.
- Do NOT propose a plan that requires 3x current capacity without a resource plan. Unfunded ambition is a setup for failure.
- Do NOT use themes that sound strategic but have no content under them. "Thought Leadership" with zero pieces assigned is a placeholder, not a pillar.
- Do NOT skip the distribution plan. Creating content without a distribution plan is like building a store on a dead-end street.
- Do NOT present vanity metrics as success criteria. "We will publish 20 blog posts" is an output metric, not a success metric. "Content will drive 200 trial signups" is a success metric.
- Do NOT copy last quarter's plan with new dates. Each quarter should reflect new learnings, market changes, and business priorities.
- Do NOT present a roadmap without the prior quarter's results. Stakeholders will ask "how did last quarter go?" first.
- Do NOT overcommit. It is better to commit to 12 excellent pieces than 30 mediocre ones. Quality compounds; volume does not.
- Do NOT ignore seasonality. Q4 content needs to account for holidays, budget cycles, and year-end behavior. Q1 needs to account for "new year" planning behavior.
- Do NOT present the roadmap as final. Build in 15-20% buffer for reactive content (trending topics, competitive responses, executive requests). A roadmap with zero flexibility will break by week 3.
- Do NOT forget to include asks. If the plan requires a budget increase, a new tool, or cross-functional support, the presentation is the time to request it.
- Do NOT build the plan in isolation. Content that does not align with product launches, sales priorities, and company initiatives will feel disconnected to stakeholders.

## Examples

# Nexus Analytics — Content Roadmap: Q3 2026

**Prepared by:** Priya Sharma, Content Marketing Lead
**Date:** 2026-06-28
**Audience:** Marketing leadership team, VP Product, VP Sales

## Content Vision

Q3 shifts from brand awareness to demand generation. Q2 established Nexus as a credible voice in product analytics — we published 14 pieces, grew organic traffic 38%, and built the email list to 4,200 subscribers. This quarter, we build the conversion layer: comparison guides, ROI tools, and customer stories that move the 40% of website visitors who read 2+ articles but never start a trial. The goal is to increase content-attributed trial starts from 85/month to 150/month.

## Last Quarter in Review

| Metric | Q2 Goal | Q2 Actual | % of Goal | Trend |
|--------|---------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| Organic Traffic (monthly) | 28,000 | 31,200 | 111% | Up |
| Email Subscribers | 3,800 | 4,200 | 111% | Up |
| Content-Attributed Trials | 100 | 85 | 85% | Flat |
| Avg. Time on Page | 3:30 | 4:12 | 120% | Up |

### Key Learnings from Last Quarter
- **Comparison content converts 3.2x better than thought leadership.** The "Nexus vs. Amplitude" guide drove 34 trial starts — more than our top 5 thought leadership pieces combined. Tripling comparison content in Q3.
- **Newsletter drives the most engaged segment.** Subscribers who come through the newsletter have 2.1x higher trial start rate than organic search visitors. Investing in newsletter growth as a conversion channel, not just a distribution channel.
- **Video underperformed expectations.** Two product walkthrough videos averaged 420 views each — well below the 2,000 target. The production cost ($2,400 per video) was not justified. Pausing video production in Q3 and redirecting budget to written content and paid distribution.

## Customization

Replace all bracket sections with your actual product, team, and content details. The themes should reflect your actual strategic priorities. The calendar should contain real or planned content pieces. Adjust the distribution channels to match where your audience actually spends time. The resource and budget sections should reflect your actual constraints and asks.

This document works best when presented live to stakeholders, then shared as a reference. Update the calendar monthly as pieces move from planned to published. Use the success metrics section as the scorecard for the quarterly content retrospective.
